The Cognitive Revolution

Cognition refers to all the processes by which the sensory input is transformed, reduced, elaborated, stored, recovered, and used.
— Ulric Neisser, Cognitive Psychology (1967)
1956

The "Magical Year" — George Miller published "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two" on memory limits. Noam Chomsky delivered his devastating review of Skinner's Verbal Behavior, proving language cannot be explained by conditioning alone. Newell & Simon unveiled the Logic Theorist — the first AI program.

1958

Donald Broadbent published Perception and Communication, introducing the filter model of attention — the first information-processing model of the mind.

1960

Jerome Bruner founded the Center for Cognitive Studies at Harvard with George Miller. The term "cognitive psychology" began gaining traction in academic circles.

1967

Ulric Neisser published the book Cognitive Psychology — the first textbook to use the term, officially naming and defining the field. He is widely considered the "father of cognitive psychology."

1969

Allan Paivio introduced the dual-coding theory, showing that visual and verbal information are processed through separate but interconnected systems.

1970s–80s

The field exploded with models of working memory (Baddeley & Hitch), schema theory (Bartlett), cognitive neuroscience (brain imaging), and connectionism (parallel distributed processing).

1990s–Present

Cognitive psychology merged with neuroscience to become cognitive neuroscience. fMRI and EEG allow real-time observation of brain activity during mental tasks. AI and machine learning continue to mirror and inform cognitive models.

The Pioneers of Cognitive Psychology

Ulric Neisser
1928 — 2012

The "father of cognitive psychology." Born in Germany, raised in the US. His 1967 book Cognitive Psychology gave the field its name and identity. Later became a critic of his own field, arguing for ecological validity — studying cognition in real-world contexts, not just laboratories.

George A. Miller
1920 — 2012

His 1956 paper "The Magical Number Seven" became one of the most cited papers in psychology. Co-founded the Harvard Center for Cognitive Studies with Bruner. Pioneered the study of working memory capacity and later helped develop WordNet, a lexical database for AI.

Noam Chomsky
1928 — Present

A linguist who delivered the fatal blow to behaviorism. His 1959 review of Skinner's Verbal Behavior proved that language acquisition requires an innate biological endowment — the Language Acquisition Device (LAD). His generative grammar revolutionized both linguistics and psychology.

Jerome Bruner
1915 — 2016

Co-founded the Harvard Center for Cognitive Studies. Pioneered research on perception, cognition, and education. His work on constructivist learning — that learners actively construct knowledge rather than passively receive it — transformed education theory worldwide.

Allen Newell & Herbert Simon
1927–1992 & 1916–2001

Created the Logic Theorist (1956) — the first AI program to mimic human problem-solving. Won the Nobel Prize in Economics (Simon, 1978) for work on bounded rationality. Their physical symbol system hypothesis claimed that intelligence requires symbol manipulation — a foundational idea for both AI and cognitive science.

Donald Broadbent
1926 — 1993

British psychologist who created the first comprehensive information-processing model of attention. His "filter model" proposed that the mind selects information early in processing. His 1958 book Perception and Communication laid the groundwork for all subsequent attention research.

What is Cognitive Psychology?

The mind is what the brain does.
— Popular cognitive science maxim

Cognitive psychology is the scientific study of mental processes — how people perceive, learn, remember, think, reason, problem-solve, and use language. It emerged as a direct rebellion against behaviorism's refusal to study the mind, using the computer as a metaphor for human information processing.

🖥️ The Computer Metaphor

The mind is like a computer: it receives input (sensory data), processes it (attention, memory, thinking), and produces output (behavior). This made the mind scientifically tractable for the first time since behaviorism.

📥 Information Processing

Cognition is a flow of information through stages: encoding → storage → retrieval. Each stage can be measured, modeled, and tested experimentally.

🧬 Mental Representations

The mind creates internal symbols, images, and schemas that stand for objects and ideas in the world. These representations are the raw material of thought.

⚙️ Cognitive Architecture

The mind has a fixed structure with specialized components: sensory registers, short-term memory, long-term memory, executive control. Each has limited capacity and specific functions.

The Core Insight: The mind can be studied scientifically — not through introspection (Wundt) or ignored (Watson), but through experiments that reveal its hidden machinery.

Core Concepts & Models

🧠 The Atkinson-Shiffrin Model

The classic three-store model: sensory memory → short-term memory → long-term memory. Information flows through each stage, with rehearsal determining what gets stored permanently.

📊 Working Memory (Baddeley)

A more dynamic model replacing "short-term memory." Includes the phonological loop (verbal), visuospatial sketchpad (visual), episodic buffer (integration), and the central executive (control).

🎯 Selective Attention

Broadbent's filter model and later Treisman's attenuation model explain how we focus on relevant stimuli while filtering out distractions — the "cocktail party effect."

🗣️ Language & Chomsky

Humans possess an innate universal grammar and a Language Acquisition Device (LAD). Children don't learn language through imitation — they generate sentences they've never heard.

💡 Problem-Solving & Heuristics

Humans use mental shortcuts (heuristics) like availability, representativeness, and anchoring. Kahneman & Tversky showed these lead to systematic biases — the foundation of behavioral economics.

🧩 Schemas & Scripts

Bartlett showed memory is reconstructive, not reproductive. We organize knowledge into schemas (mental frameworks) and scripts (expected event sequences) that shape what we remember and expect.

🔗 Connectionism (PDP)

The brain processes information in parallel distributed networks of simple units, not sequential symbol manipulation. This "neural network" approach bridges cognitive psychology and neuroscience.

🧪 Cognitive Neuroscience

Modern fMRI, PET, and EEG allow us to see the brain in action during cognitive tasks. The field has localized functions like working memory (prefrontal cortex) and face recognition (fusiform gyrus).

Cognitive Psychology vs. Behaviorism

Aspect Cognitive Psychology Behaviorism (Watson/Skinner)
Subject Matter Mental processes: memory, attention, thinking, language Observable behavior only — mind is a black box
Metaphor The mind is a computer (information processor) The organism is a machine (S-R device)
Method Reaction time, memory tasks, brain imaging, computer modeling Conditioning, reinforcement schedules, stimulus control
View of Mind The mind is active, structured, and computational The mind is irrelevant — only behavior matters
Language Innate biological endowment (Chomsky) — generative grammar Verbal behavior shaped by reinforcement (Skinner)
Learning Involves internal representations, schemas, and restructuring Stimulus-response associations through reinforcement
Nature vs. Nurture Interactionist — innate structures + environmental input Extreme nurture — environment shapes everything
Legacy Dominant paradigm today; merged with neuroscience and AI Behavior therapy; faded as dominant theoretical framework

Legacy & Modern Influence

We are at the beginning of a new era in psychology — one in which the mind, banished for half a century, has returned to claim its rightful place.
— Howard Gardner, The Mind's New Science (1985)
  • Cognitive Neuroscience: The marriage of cognitive psychology with brain imaging (fMRI, EEG) has created one of the fastest-growing fields in science, mapping mental processes to neural circuits.
  • Artificial Intelligence: Cognitive psychology and AI are deeply intertwined. Neural networks, natural language processing, and machine learning all draw on cognitive models of memory, attention, and learning.
  • Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT): Aaron Beck merged cognitive theory with behaviorist techniques to create CBT — now the most evidence-based and widely practiced form of psychotherapy worldwide.
  • Education: Constructivist learning, active recall, spaced repetition, and multimedia learning principles all derive from cognitive research. They have revolutionized teaching and EdTech.
  • Human-Computer Interaction (HCI): Cognitive psychology informs UX design, interface layout, and human factors engineering — ensuring technology aligns with how the mind actually works.
  • Behavioral Economics: Kahneman & Tversky's heuristics-and-biases research, rooted in cognitive psychology, won a Nobel Prize and transformed economics, marketing, and public policy.
  • Forensic Psychology: Cognitive research on eyewitness testimony, false memories, and interrogation techniques has changed how courts evaluate evidence and how police conduct interviews.
  • The Future: Cognitive psychology continues to evolve with computational psychiatry, brain-computer interfaces, and AI alignment — ensuring the study of the mind remains at the cutting edge of science.
Cognitive psychology did not just bring the mind back into science — it showed that the mind, like the universe, can be mapped, measured, and understood.
